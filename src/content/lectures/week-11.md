## The dashboard after the date

The dashboard has four labels. None of them is “probably into you.” This week builds a **Follow-up evaluation**: observation rules, a time-allocation critique, a frozen benchmark result, two separate sensitivity cases and a reproducible static release candidate. A disappointing result belongs in the record.

By the end, you should be able to reconstruct a label at a cutoff, defend a denominator, check the units of an ROI claim, reproduce a conservative rank, distinguish changing weights from changing evidence, and verify that the released text is the text evaluated. All records, values, ratings and probabilities are **authored fictional teaching data**. No personal experiment or private conversation is required.

## Define the second-date metric

A first date supplies sparse observations, not a labelled training set. “I had a nice time” records words. It does not provide an explicit next-date response. If a clarification is appropriate and wanted, draft one optional question such as “Would you like another board-game meetup? Fine to pass.” No response is owed, and the workbook sends nothing.

| Coded evidence at a declared cutoff | Label | What it permits you to report |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Both distinct actors explicitly agree | agreed | Agreement was recorded; attendance at another meeting is not established. |
| Either declines or withdraws | declined | Preserve the boundary; do not retry this invitation. |
| Only one agreement recorded | pending | Another agreement is missing, not secretly negative. |
| No relevant coded observation | unobserved | A missing observation, not a mood or inferred intention. |

Meeting status is a separate field. **Meeting ended + next date agreed** is coherent. A later withdrawal must remain visible even if an earlier snapshot said agreed. In Week 10's bounded event exercise, a decline is terminal and the meeting stops accepting events when it ends. This workbook reconstructs snapshots from that retained history; it does not add a live post-meeting messaging system.

### Exercise: the cutoff falls between two replies

Allow **5 minutes**. In the actual Week 10 authored trace, Alex agrees at minute **1085**, the counterpart at **1086**, and the meeting ends at **1090**. The clock is minutes since midnight on the fictional 7 May scenario. Define the window as **[0, 1086)**. Report the label at the cutoff and the latest label in the completed export.

**Debrief.** The cutoff view is **pending**, with only event `next-a`. An event exactly at 1086 falls outside the half-open window. The latest export is **agreed**, with **meeting: ended**. Keep both; a late observation must not silently rewrite a frozen reporting window. In the withdrawal branch, the label before 1087 is agreed, while before 1088 it is declined. The full event history stays attached.

An observation window is a reporting convention, not a recommended waiting time. Closing your own follow-up does not prove the other person's refusal. Coding a personal closure would require its own field, as Week 7 already demonstrates.

### Exercise: improve the percentage without improving anything

Allow **4 minutes**. A **separate** synthetic dashboard contains eight distinct records at a common rule of 48 hours after each meeting: two agreed, two declined, three pending and one unobserved. Calculate the agreement fraction among all records and among resolved records.

**Debrief.** The answers are **2/8 = 25%** and **2/(2 + 2) = 50%**. Both calculations are defined, but they answer different questions. Excluding the four unresolved records doubles the headline without adding an agreement. Report all four counts and the cutoff. An all-pending dashboard has an undefined resolved-only fraction, represented as `null`, rather than zero. These eight authored rows estimate no population rate, treatment effect or success probability.

## Key concept: return on investment

**Calculating Return on Investment (ROI) of time spent vs. romantic viability** is a critique of the objective, not an instruction to price another person. This course's benefit/time proxy allocates **Alex's own time**. The invented “benefit” does not measure romantic viability.

The separate time log contains **90 minutes together, 20 minutes of travel and five minutes composing a follow-up: 115 minutes**. The two travel legs each take ten minutes, including waiting and walking. Composition time establishes neither sending nor delivery. This fixture is distinct from Week 9's 90-minute itinerary and its 50-minute meeting. Markov steps have no minute duration.

| Activity | Half-open elapsed interval | Minutes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Outbound travel | [0, 10) | 10 |
| Time together | [10, 100) | 90 |
| Return travel | [100, 110) | 10 |
| Compose follow-up | [110, 115) | 5 |
| Total | [0, 115) | **115** |

**Exercise — 4 minutes.** A revision adds five minutes composing while on the return bus at [105, 110), but retains the full ten-minute return journey. Can you add both durations to report elapsed time? What if an interval is missing?

**Debrief.** Those activities overlap. Their effort durations can be separately described, but summing them double-counts elapsed time. This bounded workbook rejects overlapping intervals so the assumption is visible. Adjacent endpoints are accepted. Gaps are reported as unallocated time; they are not silently charged to the meeting. Duplicate IDs, negative endpoints and backwards intervals fail.

### The number changes with the invented benefit

| Assumed benefit units | Benefit / 115 minutes |
| --- | --- |
| 0 | 0 |
| 5 | 0.043478 units/minute |
| 10 | **0.086957 units/minute** |
| 20 | 0.173913 units/minute |

10/115 ≈ **0.087 units/minute** is defined arithmetic. It is about **5.217 units/hour**, not 8.7%. A conventional net-return expression `(benefit − cost) / cost` would require benefit and cost in compatible units; subtracting minutes from arbitrary benefit units is invalid. An explicit conversion could make a different toy model, but its time price would be another assumption. Zero time produces an undefined rate (`null`), not infinite viability.

**Exercise — 4 minutes.** Halve the assumed benefit, then double it. Has the observation record changed? Would reducing the recorded time by omitting travel improve the situation?

**Debrief.** The proxy halves or doubles because its numerator was chosen. Omitting travel improves the reported ratio by changing the accounting boundary. Neither operation adds mutual interest. There is no justified universal threshold for continuation.

## Past effort does not authorise the next action

Reuse [Week 7's decision comparison](/lectures/week-07/): `U(stop)=0`, `U(wait)=2q−1`, and, **only when separately admissible**, `U(clarify)=7q−5`. The parameter q and the five-unit clarification cost are assumptions on Week 7's utility scale. Five units are not the five composition minutes. Do not estimate q from our tiny dashboard, infer it from “nice time,” or substitute the Markov N probability.

| Assumed q | Stop | Wait | Clarify, if admissible | Maximiser |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0.3 | 0 | −0.4 | −2.9 | stop |
| 0.5 | 0 | 0 | −1.5 | stop and wait |
| 0.8 | 0 | 0.6 | 0.6 | wait and clarify |
| 0.9 | 0 | 0.8 | 1.3 | clarify |

**Exercise — 5 minutes.** Derive the stop/wait crossover and the wait/clarify crossover. At q=0.9, remove clarification from the admissible set. Then add an explicit refusal.

**Debrief.** `2q−1=0` gives **q=0.5**. `7q−5=2q−1` gives **q=0.8**. With clarification excluded at 0.9, wait is the numerical maximiser among the remaining actions. An explicit refusal terminates the invitation before numerical optimisation: **stop**. The generic comparison function's admissibility flag is not a complete consent protocol. The earlier terminal state rules still apply. Subtracting the same already-incurred time cost from every future option would not change their ordering; it cannot create permission to continue.

| Feature | Authored rating | Evidence and limitation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Clarity | 4 | “maybe a relationship?” makes the supported intent explicit. |
| Specificity | 4 | “Board games, bad puns” uses concrete case facts. |

A quote-presence check can catch a missing quote. It cannot decide whether a human rating is justified.

## Primary score and the conservative tie rule

Null Island v1 contains **99 unchanged synthetic controls plus one candidate = 100 entries**. It is an authored comparator, not representative app data or a held-out user study.

```text
score = 100 × (clarity + specificity + feasibility + exit) / 16
rank = 1 + count(control score >= candidate score)
```

For (4,4,3,4), the sum is 15 and the score is **93.75**. No control scores higher, but one control ties: **rank = 1 + 0 + 1 = 2/100**. Only rank 1 receives the benchmark's top-1% label. Ties count against the candidate, so a `>` comparison is wrong. Do not round scores before ranking.

**Exercise — 6 minutes.** Manually score control `control-099`=(3,4,4,4) and compare it with the worked candidate. A colleague reports “joint first, therefore top 1%.” Identify the failed contract.

**Debrief.** Both total 15 and score 93.75. The course uses a conservative rank, not a joint-first convention. The target fails. Rank is not a marking criterion; explaining this failed target can support an excellent evaluation. The ideal (4,4,4,4) scores 100 and ranks first in this deliberately constructed fixture, but ratings require evidence.

## Change the weight for every entry

Double feasibility's weight without changing the candidate or any evidence:

```text
weighted score = 100 × (clarity + specificity + 2×feasibility + exit) / 20
```

The maximum is `4+4+8+4=20`. Recompute **all 99 controls and the candidate** under the same rule. Mixing a new candidate score with old control scores compares different scales.

| Worked input | Primary score / rank | Double-feasibility score / rank |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Release example (4,4,3,4) | 93.75 / 2 | 90 / 2 |
| Separate weak-feasibility probe (4,4,1,4) | **81.25 / 5** | **70 / 22** |
| Ideal arithmetic check (4,4,4,4) | 100 / 1 | 100 / 1 |

**Exercise — 7 minutes.** Derive the probe's 81.25 and 70, then reproduce its ranks from the CSV. Count greater scores and equal scores separately. Explain why the release's unchanged rank does not imply an unchanged comparison.

**Debrief.** The probe has `13/16×100=81.25` and `14/20×100=70`. Initially one control is above and three tie, giving **5**. Under doubled feasibility, twelve are above and nine tie, giving **22**. The release example moves from one tie to one strictly better control, so its rank stays **2** even as its score falls. That control now scores **95**.

Optional extension: for weight w, the release numerator is `12+3w`; control 099's is `11+4w`. With a common positive denominator, their difference changes sign at **w=1**. Pairwise ordering changes there; the conservative tie rule still counts equality against the candidate. Changing a weight expresses a different rubric preference, not a causal dating effect.

## Change availability in a separate case

Retain the original baseline and weight-sensitivity results. Now declare a **different fictional case**: Alex has only **Friday 5–6:30 pm**, or **90 minutes**. This does not change Alex's fixed course biography; it is a labelled stress test.

The unchanged library plan still needs 100 minutes, so its slack changes from **+20 to −10 minutes**. It fails the time constraint. The unchanged bio still promises Friday 5–7 pm, contradicting the changed case. Under the published anchors, this authored review changes feasibility from 3 to **2**, leaving the other ratings unchanged. The primary score becomes **87.5, rank 2/100**. **Case fidelity fails, so release under the changed case is blocked regardless of rank.**

**Exercise — 6 minutes.** List the statements and artefacts affected. Can the unchanged rank justify retaining the invitation? Does the 90-minute Week 9 itinerary rescue the 100-minute library plan?

**Debrief.** Review the time claim, library itinerary, dependent invitations and any agreement to those exact terms. A different venue's duration cannot be substituted without changing the plan and seeking the relevant agreement. A new bio or shorter plan would be an exploratory revision with its own version and review. Do not silently repair the frozen candidate, mutate the fixed controls or overwrite the original result.

Controls are supplied numeric vectors without source profile text. We therefore keep them fixed in this **candidate-only changed-case stress test**. We cannot truthfully re-rate all controls for a new availability condition without their evidence. The doubled-weight comparison, by contrast, can and does rescore every entry. Neither exercise measures a real population distribution shift.

## Evaluation can quietly become development

[scikit-learn's leakage guidance](https://scikit-learn.org/stable/common_pitfalls.html#data-leakage) explains why test information must not influence model choices and why learned preprocessing belongs on training data. Here the analogous mistake is editing a candidate after seeing its benchmark result, then calling the new result an untouched evaluation of the old candidate. This course benchmark is public and synthetic; even a careful freeze does not make it a hidden test set.

**Exercise — 5 minutes.** Classify these actions: (a) save the primary result and run the declared weight change; (b) inspect controls, edit the bio and keep both versions as exploration; (c) delete the failed result and publish the new score as the original; (d) raise feasibility to 4 because rank 2 looks disappointing.

**Debrief.** (a) is prespecified sensitivity; (b) is labelled development; (c) misrepresents the evaluation history; (d) abandons the evidence anchors. Any real predictive claim would need appropriate independent evidence. Do not invent new people or observations to manufacture that evidence here.
